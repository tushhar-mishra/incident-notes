## ⚡ Core: the 10-minute version

**The idea.** GitHub's MySQL clusters had primaries on the US East Coast, managed by **Orchestrator**, which uses Raft consensus to decide failovers. During maintenance to replace failing optical equipment, the East Coast data center lost connectivity to the network hub for **43 seconds**. Orchestrator nodes on the West Coast and in the cloud formed a quorum and **promoted West Coast servers** as primaries. When the network came back, the app started writing to the West Coast. Now there were **East Coast writes** that never reached the West, and **West Coast writes** that never reached the East. Failing back would lose data; staying meant crippling **cross-country latency**.

```
22:52 UTC  optical maintenance → 43 s partition (East Coast DC ↔ hub)
           Orchestrator quorum (West + cloud) promotes West Coast primaries
           network returns → app writes to West Coast primaries
           East Coast: a small set of writes never replicated West   (e.g. 954 writes on one busy cluster)
           West Coast: 30+ minutes of new writes
Decision: protect data integrity → restore East Coast from backups, replicate from West, then fail back
           backups: multiple TB from remote storage, decompress/verify/load → hours
Oct 22 23:03 UTC: all backlogs processed → 24 h 11 min of degradation
```

**Picture it.** Two branch offices share a ledger; the head office keeps the master copy. The phone line drops for 43 seconds, and the rules say "if head office is silent, the other branch becomes master". When the line returns, both have written entries the other doesn't have — and the new master is across the country, so every entry now takes ages.

**The one thing to know.** **Failover automation must understand topology** (never promote across regions the app can't tolerate) and **short partitions** are the dangerous ones. And when data is at stake, **integrity beats speed**.

**You've got it if you can:** explain why GitHub couldn't simply fail back, and what made recovery take a full day.

## Summary

On 21 October 2018, routine maintenance to replace failing 100G optical equipment cut connectivity between GitHub's US East Coast network hub and its primary East Coast data center for 43 seconds. GitHub's Orchestrator, using Raft consensus, promoted West Coast database primaries; after connectivity returned, applications wrote to the West Coast while some East Coast writes had not been replicated. To protect data integrity GitHub restored East Coast databases from backups, resynchronised replicas and failed back, causing 24 hours and 11 minutes of service degradation.

## Impact

| Item | Detail (from GitHub's analysis) |
|---|---|
| Duration | **24 hours 11 minutes** of degradation (22:52 UTC 21 Oct → 23:03 UTC 22 Oct) |
| Features | Out-of-date and inconsistent information displayed; for most of the incident, **webhooks** and **GitHub Pages** builds unavailable |
| Backlog | **Over five million** webhook events and **80,000** Pages builds queued; about **200,000** webhook payloads dropped after exceeding an internal TTL |
| Data | "no user data was lost"; manual reconciliation of a few seconds of writes in progress |
| Customers / cost | **Not publicly disclosed** |

## How it happened

```
          US East Coast hub ══╳══ East Coast primary DC (MySQL primaries)    ← 43-second break
                 │
   Orchestrator (Raft): nodes in West Coast DC + East Coast public cloud = quorum
                 │  "primary unreachable" → promote West Coast replicas to primary
                 ▼
          West Coast DC: new primaries ◄── app writes (after network returns)
          East Coast DC: old primaries had a few writes not yet replicated West
          → two diverging histories; the app tier can't run with cross-country write latency
```

GitHub: Orchestrator's "actions behaved as configured", but the configuration allowed promoting primaries **across regions**, a topology the application tier "could not support". "Leader-election within a region is generally safe, but the sudden introduction of cross-country latency was a major contributing factor."

## Why it spread

- **Automation acted on a short partition** that it couldn't distinguish from a real failure.
- **Cross-region primaries** made writes slow for the East Coast application.
- **Diverged data** prevented a quick fail-back without losing writes.
- **Restore time:** clusters up to nearly five terabytes; backups taken every four hours and stored remotely; transferring, decompressing, verifying and loading took hours, and replica catch-up was slower than predicted.

## How they found it

Internal monitoring alerted at 22:54 (two minutes after the partition). Responders then saw from the Orchestrator API that cluster topologies contained only West Coast servers (23:02).

## What they did to fix it

1. **Locked deployments** and **paused webhooks and Pages builds** to avoid making data inconsistency worse.
2. Chose to **fail forward**: restore East Coast databases from backups, replicate from the West Coast, then move primaries back — accepting a longer outage to protect data integrity.
3. Added read replicas in the public cloud to spread read load during catch-up.
4. **Failed back** to the original topology once replicas were in sync (16:24).
5. Processed the backlog; raised the webhook TTL after some payloads were dropped.
6. Captured binary logs of the East Coast writes that weren't replicated to reconcile them (automatically where possible, with user outreach otherwise).

## Permanent fix (as listed by GitHub)

| Change | Pattern it addresses |
|---|---|
| Orchestrator configured to **prevent promotion of primaries across regional boundaries** | Topology-unaware failover |
| **Component-level status reporting** instead of only green/yellow/red | Communication |
| **Active/active/active** multi-data-center design with N+1 facility-level redundancy (accelerated) | Single-region primaries |
| Test assumptions proactively; invest in **fault injection and chaos engineering** | Untested failure modes |

## Follow-up questions

1. **Q: What was the real root cause?**
   A: Failover automation configured to allow promoting primaries across regions, which a 43-second partition triggered; the app couldn't tolerate cross-country writes, and unreplicated writes on both sides made a quick fail-back unsafe.

2. **Q: How was it detected?**
   A: Monitoring alerted two minutes after the partition; topology checks showed only West Coast servers in the clusters.

3. **Q: What did they check first?**
   A: Database cluster topology via Orchestrator — the state of primaries determines every next step.

4. **Q: How did they stop the impact from getting worse?**
   A: Locked deployments and paused webhooks/Pages to avoid more writes and inconsistent side effects.

5. **Q: Why not simply fail back to the East Coast?**
   A: The West Coast had 30+ minutes of new writes and the East Coast had some unreplicated writes. Failing back would have lost or conflicted data; GitHub prioritised integrity.

6. **Q: Why did it take 24 hours?**
   A: Restoring terabytes from remote backups, verifying and loading, then replicas catching up slowly, then processing millions of queued events.

7. **Q: What changed permanently?**
   A: No cross-region primary promotion, component-level status, active/active/active work, and chaos engineering.

8. **Q: What if restoring from backups had failed?**
   A: They'd have had to run with West Coast primaries (slow but consistent) while rebuilding, or accept data reconciliation risks. Backups tested daily made the chosen path viable.

9. **Q: How would you prevent this class of incident?**
   A: Topology-aware failover, dampening against short partitions, and regular partition testing.

10. **Q: What's the lesson about "the automation behaved as configured"?**
    A: Automation is only as safe as its configuration's assumptions. Review failover rules against what the application can actually tolerate, not just what's technically possible.
